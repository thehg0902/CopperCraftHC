What a Finished CopperCraft Job Looks Like

Most of an HVAC installation is invisible once it is done. That is exactly why the finish is the part worth judging.

  • What to look for in these photos

    • Pipe runs that are square and supported.
    • Service clearance left on every side of the equipment.
    • Vents terminated properly.
    • Connections a technician can reach without dismantling something else.

  • Workmanship is not decoration. It decides performance.

    • A condenser crowded against a fence runs hotter and fails sooner.
    • A tankless heater on an undersized gas line never reaches its rated output.
    • Uninsulated duct in an unconditioned space gives away the temperature you just paid to produce.

  • The part you cannot inspect afterwards

    The install decides how the equipment performs for the rest of its life. It is also the part a homeowner is least equipped to check once the panel is back on.

    • Same standard on a straight furnace replacement.
    • Same standard on a first-time central air installation.
    • Same standard on a light commercial unit.

  • Air-Conditioning Condenser

    Air Conditioning

    Set on a level pad with clearance on every side, which is what lets the unit reject heat properly instead of recirculating its own exhaust. The line set is run tight to the wall and the service gauges are connected for the charge check that finishes a proper install.

  • Tankless Water Heater

    Water Heaters

    Wall-mounted to free the floor, with the copper water lines and the gas run brought in square and supported. Tankless units need adequate gas supply and correct venting — the pipework is the part that decides whether the unit performs to spec.

  • Gas Water Heater

    Water Heaters

    A power-vented tank with the vent run up to the ceiling and away cleanly. Power venting is what allows a tank to go where a natural-draft chimney is not available, which is common in finished basements.

  • Mechanical Room

    Indoor Comfort

    Twin water softeners, a wall-mounted boiler and insulated ducting sharing one room. Layout matters here: every unit needs service clearance, and insulated duct on the supply side keeps conditioned air from losing its temperature before it reaches the house.

  • Commercial Water Heaters

    Light Commercial

    Three units banked side by side to meet a demand a single tank could not carry. Sequencing several smaller heaters keeps hot water available if one goes down, which is the reason light commercial jobs are often built this way.

  • Rooftop Packaged Unit

    Light Commercial

    A packaged rooftop unit — heating and cooling in one cabinet, sited on a curb on a flat roof. Common on storefronts and small commercial units because it keeps mechanical equipment out of leasable floor space.
