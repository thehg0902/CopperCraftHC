Energy Recovery Ventilators (ERV)

The choice between the two devices is not about quality. It is about which direction your house gets the humidity wrong.

The case for moisture transfer

  • Winter air that leaves the house is humid; an ERV keeps some of that moisture instead of exhausting all of it.
  • Homes that go uncomfortably dry in January — static, cracked woodwork, sore throats — are the ones an ERV suits.
  • In summer it works the other way, holding back some of the humidity that outdoor air would otherwise carry inside.

Helpful to know

  • An ERV is not a humidifier. It slows moisture loss; it does not add moisture the house never had.
  • If your windows stream with condensation each winter, you have the opposite problem and an HRV is the better tool.
  • Both types need the same ducting and roughly the same space, so the decision is worth making before installation rather than after.
  • Filters and the recovery core still need seasonal cleaning — the same maintenance an HRV asks for.

Common Questions

What is the actual difference between an ERV and an HRV?

Both exchange stale indoor air for fresh outdoor air and recover heat on the way. Only the ERV also transfers moisture between the two streams. Everything else about them — ducting, filters, installation — is broadly the same.

How do I know which one my house needs?

Look at your windows in deep winter. Heavy condensation means excess indoor moisture and points to an HRV. A house that feels parched, with static and shrinking trim, points to an ERV. We will look at the house rather than guess from the outside.

Can an ERV replace my furnace humidifier?

Not really — they do different jobs. A humidifier adds moisture; an ERV reduces how much you lose. In a very dry house you may still want both, and we will tell you if one alone would do.
