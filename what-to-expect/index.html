What a No-Heat Call Actually Involves

Heater repair in Richmond Hill is mostly diagnosis. The repair itself is often quick once the cause is known — finding the cause is the work.

When a furnace stops producing heat, the cause is usually one of a small number of things: a failed igniter or a flame sensor coated in residue, a pressure switch reacting to a blocked intake or exhaust, a thermostat that has lost power or calibration, a limit switch tripping because airflow is restricted, or a blower motor that is drawing too much current. Each of those produces a slightly different pattern of behaviour, which is why the first question is always what the system is doing rather than what part you would like replaced.

Short cycling — the furnace lighting, running briefly and shutting down again — is the most commonly misread symptom. It looks like a dying furnace and it very often is not. Restricted airflow from a clogged filter will do it. So will a return duct that was never sized for the equipment. Replacing the furnace in that situation buys a new machine that will short cycle in exactly the same way, which is not a repair anyone should be paying for.

Some things are worth checking before you call, because they cost nothing and occasionally solve it: the thermostat batteries, the furnace power switch (it looks like a light switch and gets turned off by accident more often than you would think), the breaker, and whether the filter is visibly grey. If the furnace has an inspection window, note whether it attempts to ignite at all. That single detail narrows the diagnosis considerably.

One thing is not a diagnostic question. If you smell gas, or a carbon-monoxide alarm sounds, leave the house first and call your gas utility from outside. That comes before calling us or anyone else.

Repair or Replace

The honest version of a question that gets answered dishonestly a lot.

A repair is usually the right call when the failed component is a normal wear item, the rest of the system is sound, and the unit still has years of reasonable service in it. Replacement starts making sense when the equipment is old enough that parts are getting scarce, when the efficiency gap has grown large enough to show up on the bill, or when the cost of the repair is a meaningful fraction of what a new system would cost anyway.

Nobody can tell you which of those applies over the phone, and we will not try. The system gets inspected, you get the findings, and you decide. If financing is what makes a replacement workable rather than an emergency, that is a separate conversation we are happy to have — as an option, not a push.
